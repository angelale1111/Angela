About Dr. Angela Le

Dr. Le is a Doctor of Acupuncture and Chinese Medicine, in practice since 2001, and one of the country’s early integrative fertility acupuncturists. Over decades of clinical practice, she has worked with thousands of women through health challenges and major reproductive life transitions.

Her work has continued to evolve alongside her own life, moving beyond medicine toward deeper questions of how we live, how we love, and what becomes possible when we no longer live from survival.

Dr. Le is the founder of Fifth Avenue Fertility Wellness in New York City. Her private mentorship brings together work that has been developing over many years, shaped by both her life and career.

My Story

Long before I was a clinician, I was a young woman searching for answers no one seemed to have.

At sixteen, I received a misdiagnosis that led to a cascade of medical interventions. What followed was years of struggling with my health, leaving me physically and emotionally unrecognizable to myself. I felt alone inside my experience, trying to piece together a life that no longer felt like mine.

That period changed everything. I now recognize it as the turning point. It dismantled what I thought I knew and set me on a path I could not yet see.

At twenty-two, I felt an almost inexplicable pull to India. I was searching for my purpose, and somehow I knew I would find answers there.

Through a series of almost impossible coincidences, I met a couple. Together, they carried a depth of devotion, service, and presence I had never witnessed so intimately. Their lives were rooted in something larger than themselves, and something in me knew I had found something essential.

The wife practiced Traditional Chinese Medicine, and I sensed that it was part of what supported that way of being. I knew this was a path I needed to explore.

I returned home and devoted myself to the study of Traditional Chinese Medicine, applying what I was learning to my own health. I transformed the way I lived through discipline and determination.

And I healed nearly a decade of illness.

For years, I thought healing had a destination, and I had arrived.

A car accident changed that, leaving me with chronic pain that would follow for years.

Fear of the pain became a constant threat. I began shaping my life around managing and controlling it. The more I tried, the smaller my world became.

One night, the pain brought me to a breaking point. I found myself on the bathroom floor in the dark, sobbing.

And then I heard a whisper.

Stop fixing.

Fixing was all I knew.

It had helped me reclaim my health once. It had given me a sense of agency when so much had felt beyond my control. But for the first time, I wondered whether the very strategy that had once helped me was now keeping me stuck.

Around that time, a therapist suggested that what I was experiencing might be rooted in trauma. I resisted the idea completely.

My parents had survived a war. Their suffering felt so much greater. I was not ready to acknowledge my own.

But the idea stayed with me. Over time, I began to understand that trauma was not defined only by what had happened, but by the activation the body had not been able to discharge.

Underneath all my control was a nervous system that had learned, before I had words, to stay vigilant. I had learned that safety meant being useful, anticipating what others needed, and never asking for too much.

For years, that had looked like strength.

It left very little room for trust.

I began to see the belief I had been living by:

It is all up to me.

That belief had carried me through some of the hardest years of my life. But it had also made it almost impossible to rest.

I began to understand that the very ways I had learned to survive were now asking to be released.

I could take responsibility for my life without trying to control all of it.

I could listen instead of override.

I could allow instead of resist.

I could begin to trust.

As I created the safety my nervous system had never known, something in me settled. The bracing eased. And over time, the chronic pain resolved completely.

Parts of life I had been guarding myself from began to return.

Joy.

Connection.

Presence.

Today, I experience a level of health and aliveness I did not know was possible.

What changed was my relationship with myself, with others, and with life.

Looking back, I can see that this has been the thread running through my work from the beginning. Even when I did not yet have the language for it, I was interested in more than symptoms. I was interested in what happens when a woman begins to understand herself differently, and what becomes possible from there.

Over the years, my own life and my work with women have continued to deepen that understanding. I have come to see how much of who we believe ourselves to be is shaped by the roles we take on, the beliefs we inherit, and the ways we learn to protect ourselves.

And I have seen what becomes possible when a woman begins to recognize the difference between who she is and what she learned to be.

This is where my work has brought me.

Bringing everything I have lived, studied, and learned into a deeper expression of the work I have been doing all along.

The Evolution of the Work

My foundation was Traditional Chinese Medicine and Taoist study. At Yo San University, I trained with Master Ni and his sons, heirs to a seventy-four-generation Taoist lineage, studying medicine alongside Taoist philosophy, meditation, Qi Gong, Tai Chi, and sexual practices.

Integrative health and longevity science later added another lens through which to understand physiology, resilience, and vitality.

Somatic Experiencing, Polyvagal Theory, and Internal Family Systems deepened my understanding of the nervous system, including how protective responses can continue long after the original experience has passed.

Somatic sex education expanded the work into intimacy, embodiment, pleasure, and our relationship with our own bodies. I became certified as a Somatic Sexuality Healing Practitioner (SSHP), deepening my work in these areas.

Nondual inquiry brought me into questions that cannot always be solved through effort, including questions of identity, the nature of self, and what remains when we look beyond the stories we have come to believe about ourselves.

Each discipline has shaped me.

None of them, on its own, defines the work.

What matters is the ability to draw from what is useful, without forcing a person into a particular framework.
